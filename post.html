The Life He Had to Let Go

Daniel Kiprono on flying, ambition and the life beyond the cockpit.

By Namna Staff · September 25, 2026 · 5 min read

The boy from Nakuru wanted to fly. He would stand at the edge of the school field and watch the light aircraft crossing towards the Rift Valley, and he told anyone who would listen that one day he would be up there. For the better part of three decades it looked as if the dream belonged to someone else.

“I never stopped looking up,” Daniel Kiprono says. Getting there took him the long way round: through an ordinary report card, a diploma he never planned to study, years of unpaid internships and nearly two decades in television. Then, to reach the cockpit, he let that life go.

The numbers that said no

By his own account, Kiprono was an average student at best. He was good at stories and bad at physics, and his final secondary school grade was nowhere near what a pilot training programme would ask for. “I remember folding the results slip very small, as if that would make it mean less,” he says.

Flight school was also expensive, and his family had already stretched to get him through school. The dream went into a drawer.

The fallback that became a career

A cousin suggested broadcast journalism, and Kiprono enrolled at a media college in Nairobi. Journalism was the fallback, not the plan. It turned out to be a long and serious career.

He started as an unpaid intern in a newsroom, carrying tripods and logging tapes, and was eventually given a slot presenting a weekend youth show. Over the next eighteen years he presented and produced features on travel, food and culture, and became a familiar face on Kenyan screens. “Television taught me how to tell a story in ninety seconds,” he says. “It also taught me that I was always telling other people’s stories.”

Letting go

At 40, Kiprono walked away. That is the life he had to let go: the studios, the recognition in the supermarket queue, and a working identity he had spent two decades building. He does not describe it as a loss. “I traded a microphone for a headset,” he says, laughing. “Both of them come with a lot of talking.”

“You do not need permission to start again. You just need to book the first lesson.” — Daniel Kiprono

The people closest to him were not surprised. His wife, he says, had been hearing about aeroplanes since their first date.

Wheels up

Kiprono trained at a flight school at Wilson Airport, paying for his lessons with savings and freelance production work. He flew his first solo on a clear morning in 2022 and marked it the way aviators do, soaked with buckets of water by his instructors. Today he holds a private pilot licence and is working towards a commercial one.

Beyond the cockpit

The life he let go has not disappeared. It has followed him on board. Kiprono now wants to film the country from the air, bringing the camera and the craft he learned in television into the cockpit with him. The boy from Nakuru got the career he asked for. He simply reached it by way of another one.
